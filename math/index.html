    Sine, cosine, tangent and cotangent

    These four functions compare the sides of a right triangle. Pick one of the two sharp angles and call it θ (theta). Then name each side by where it sits from θ's point of view.

    1. Name the sides from the angle

    • Opposite: the side straight across from θ. It doesn't touch θ.
    • Adjacent: the side next to θ that isn't the hypotenuse.
    • Hypotenuse: the longest side, across from the right angle. It never changes.

    If you switch to the other sharp angle, opposite and adjacent trade places. The hypotenuse stays the same.

    2. The four ratios

    sin θoppositehypotenuseSOH
    cos θadjacenthypotenuseCAH
    tan θoppositeadjacentTOA
    cot θadjacentoppositetan flipped

    SOH CAH TOA is the memory trick: Sine = Opposite over Hypotenuse, Cosine = Adjacent over Hypotenuse, Tangent = Opposite over Adjacent. Cotangent is just tangent turned upside down.

    3. Try it: move the angle

    Drag the slider. The triangle changes shape, but each ratio depends only on the angle, not on how big the triangle is.

    4. How they connect

    • tan θ = sin θ ÷ cos θ
    • cot θ = 1 ÷ tan θ = cos θ ÷ sin θ
    • sin θ = cos(90° − θ): the sine of one sharp angle is the cosine of the other.
    • cot θ = tan(90° − θ), for the same reason.
    • sin and cos are always between 0 and 1 in a right triangle, because the hypotenuse is the longest side.

    Cotangent has two cousins you may see later: cosecant (csc = 1 ÷ sin = hyp/opp) and secant (sec = 1 ÷ cos = hyp/adj).

    5. Worked examples

    A. Write all four ratios

    A right triangle has opposite = 6, adjacent = 8 and hypotenuse = 10.

    1. sin θ = 6/10 = 3/5
    2. cos θ = 8/10 = 4/5
    3. tan θ = 6/8 = 3/4
    4. cot θ = 8/6 = 4/3

    B. Find a missing side

    θ = 35° and the hypotenuse is 12. Find the opposite side, x.

    1. You have the hypotenuse and want the opposite, so use SOH.
    2. sin 35° = x / 12
    3. x = 12 × sin 35° = 12 × 0.5736
    4. x ≈ 6.9

    C. Find a missing angle

    The opposite side is 5 and the adjacent side is 9. Find θ.

    1. Opposite and adjacent → TOA.
    2. tan θ = 5/9 ≈ 0.5556
    3. Use the inverse key: θ = tan⁻¹(0.5556)
    4. θ ≈ 29.1°

    D. Use cotangent

    θ = 40° and the opposite side is 7. Find the adjacent side, x.

    1. Adjacent over opposite is cotangent: cot 40° = x / 7
    2. Most calculators have no cot key, so use 1 ÷ tan: cot 40° = 1 ÷ 0.8391 ≈ 1.1918
    3. x = 7 × 1.1918
    4. x ≈ 8.3

    6. Special angles worth memorizing

    θsincostancot
    30°1/2√3/2√3/3√3
    45°√2/2√2/211
    60°√3/21/2√3√3/3

    Notice the pattern: sin 30° = cos 60° and tan 30° = cot 60°. Complementary angles swap.

    7. Calculator tips and common mistakes

    • Put your calculator in degree mode (DEG), not radians. sin 30° should show 0.5.
    • No cot button? Type 1 ÷ tan. For the angle, cot⁻¹(x) = tan⁻¹(1 ÷ x).
    • Use sin⁻¹, cos⁻¹ or tan⁻¹ (often 2nd or Shift) to find an angle from a ratio.
    • Name sides from the angle you're using, not from the picture's position.
    • Don't flip a ratio: sin is over the hypotenuse, never under it.
    • If the unknown is on the bottom of the fraction, you divide (x = 7 ÷ sin θ), not multiply.

    8. Quick check

    A right triangle has opposite = 5, adjacent = 12 and hypotenuse = 13. Tap each one to check yourself.

    sin θ = ?

    5/13

    cos θ = ?

    12/13

    tan θ = ?

    5/12

    cot θ = ?

    12/5

    θ to the nearest tenth?

    tan⁻¹(5/12) ≈ 22.6°

    Quadratic equations

    A quadratic equation has an x² term and can be written as ax² + bx + c = 0 (a can't be 0). Its graph is a U-shaped curve called a parabola. The solutions, also called roots, are where the parabola crosses the x-axis. There can be two, one or none.

    1. See it: move a, b and c

    Positive a opens up; negative a opens down. Slide c up and watch the roots disappear when the curve leaves the x-axis.

    2. Method 1: factoring

    If the left side factors, set each factor equal to 0. This works because if two things multiply to 0, one of them must be 0 (the zero product property).

    1. x² − 5x + 6 = 0
    2. Two numbers that multiply to 6 and add to −5: −2 and −3
    3. (x − 2)(x − 3) = 0
    4. x − 2 = 0 or x − 3 = 0
    5. x = 2 or x = 3

    Always get 0 alone on one side first. x² − 5x = −6 must become x² − 5x + 6 = 0.

    3. Method 2: square roots

    Use this when there's no plain x term, or the equation is already a square. Remember both the positive and negative root.

    1. (x − 3)² = 16
    2. x − 3 = ±4
    3. x = 3 + 4 or x = 3 − 4
    4. x = 7 or x = −1

    4. Method 3: the quadratic formula

    x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) ÷ 2a

    It works on every quadratic, even ones that won't factor.

    Example: 2x² + 3x − 5 = 0

    1. a = 2, b = 3, c = −5
    2. b² − 4ac = 9 − (−40) = 49
    3. x = (−3 ± √49) ÷ 4 = (−3 ± 7) ÷ 4
    4. x = 1 or x = −2.5

    Example that doesn't factor: x² + 4x − 1 = 0

    1. a = 1, b = 4, c = −1
    2. b² − 4ac = 16 + 4 = 20
    3. x = (−4 ± √20) ÷ 2 = −2 ± √5
    4. x ≈ 0.24 or x ≈ −4.24

    5. The discriminant

    The part under the square root, b² − 4ac, tells you how many real solutions there are before you solve.

    • Positive: 2 solutions (crosses the x-axis twice)
    • Zero: 1 solution (just touches the x-axis)
    • Negative: no real solutions (never reaches the x-axis)

    6. The vertex

    The vertex is the parabola's turning point: its lowest point if it opens up, highest if it opens down.

    1. y = x² − 6x + 5
    2. x = −b ÷ 2a = 6 ÷ 2 = 3
    3. y = 3² − 6(3) + 5 = −4
    4. Vertex (3, −4)

    7. Which method should I use?

    • Easy numbers that factor → factoring (fastest)
    • Something squared = a number → square roots
    • Anything else, or you're stuck → quadratic formula (always works)

    8. Quick check

    Solve x² + x − 12 = 0

    (x + 4)(x − 3) = 0, so x = −4 or x = 3

    How many solutions does x² + 2x + 5 = 0 have?

    b² − 4ac = 4 − 20 = −16, so none

    Vertex of y = 2x² + 8x + 1?

    x = −8 ÷ 4 = −2; y = 8 − 16 + 1 = −7 → (−2, −7)

    Teaching · Algebra

    Factoring

    Factoring is multiplying in reverse: you break an expression into pieces that multiply together. Just as 12 = 3 × 4, the expression x² + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3). You can always check your answer by multiplying it back out.

    1. Always start with the GCF

    Pull out the greatest common factor: the biggest number and lowest power of x that every term shares.

    1. 6x³ + 9x²
    2. GCF of 6 and 9 is 3; lowest power of x is x²
    3. Divide each term by 3x²: 2x and 3
    4. 3x²(2x + 3)

    2. Trinomials: x² + bx + c

    Find two numbers that multiply to c and add to b. They go in the two parentheses.

    1. x² + 7x + 12
    2. Pairs that multiply to 12: 1·12, 2·6, 3·4. Which adds to 7? 3 and 4
    3. (x + 3)(x + 4)
    • c positive → both numbers have the same sign as b
    • c negative → one positive, one negative; the bigger one takes b's sign
    1. x² − 2x − 15
    2. Multiply to −15, add to −2: −5 and 3
    3. (x − 5)(x + 3)

    3. Try it: the number finder

    Enter b and c from x² + bx + c. Every pair that multiplies to c is listed, and the one that adds to b lights up.

    4. Difference of squares

    a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b)

    1. x² − 49 = x² − 7² = (x − 7)(x + 7)
    2. 4x² − 25 = (2x)² − 5² = (2x − 5)(2x + 5)

    A sum of squares, like x² + 49, does not factor with real numbers.

    5. When there's a number in front: ax² + bx + c

    Use the a·c method: multiply a × c, find two numbers that multiply to it and add to b, split the middle term, then group.

    1. 2x² + 7x + 3
    2. a·c = 2 × 3 = 6. Multiply to 6, add to 7: 6 and 1
    3. Split: 2x² + 6x + x + 3
    4. Group: 2x(x + 3) + 1(x + 3)
    5. (2x + 1)(x + 3)

    6. Perfect squares

    If the first and last terms are squares and the middle is twice their product, it's a perfect square.

    1. x² + 6x + 9 = (x + 3)²
    2. x² − 10x + 25 = (x − 5)²

    7. Factoring checklist

    1. Take out the GCF.
    2. Two terms? Look for a difference of squares.
    3. Three terms? Use the number finder (a = 1) or the a·c method.
    4. Check: multiply your answer back out.
    5. Look again: can any piece factor further?

    8. Quick check

    x² + 9x + 20

    (x + 4)(x + 5)

    x² − 64

    (x − 8)(x + 8)

    5x² − 20

    5(x² − 4) = 5(x − 2)(x + 2)

    3x² + 10x + 8

    a·c = 24 → 6 and 4: 3x(x + 2) + 4(x + 2) = (3x + 4)(x + 2)

    Teaching · Probability

    Probability

    Probability measures how likely something is, on a scale from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). You can write it as a fraction, a decimal or a percent: 1/4 = 0.25 = 25%.

    1. The basic formula

    P(event) = favorable outcomes ÷ total outcomes

    1. Roll one die. What is P(even)?
    2. Favorable: 2, 4, 6 → 3 outcomes. Total: 6
    3. P = 3/6 = 1/2

    Complement: the chance something does not happen is 1 minus the chance it does. P(not 6) = 1 − 1/6 = 5/6.

    2. Try it: a bag of marbles

    Real results wobble around the math. The more draws you make, the closer they get.

    3. "And": multiply

    For two things that both happen, multiply their probabilities.

    1. Flip a coin and roll a die. P(heads and 6)?
    2. 1/2 × 1/6 = 1/12

    With or without replacement

    A bag has 3 red and 2 blue. Draw two. P(both red)?

    1. Put the first one back: 3/5 × 3/5 = 9/25
    2. Keep the first one out: 3/5 × 2/4 = 6/20 = 3/10 (one fewer red, one fewer total)

    4. "Or": add

    If the events can't happen together, add them. P(roll a 1 or a 2) = 1/6 + 1/6 = 1/3.

    If they can overlap, subtract the overlap so it isn't counted twice:

    P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B)

    1. Draw one card. P(heart or king)?
    2. 13/52 + 4/52 − 1/52 (the king of hearts)
    3. = 16/52 = 4/13

    5. Two dice

    Two dice have 6 × 6 = 36 outcomes. Each cell is the total of the two dice. 7 shows up most: 6 times, so P(7) = 6/36 = 1/6.

    6. Counting outcomes

    • Multiply choices: 3 shirts × 4 pants = 12 outfits
    • Arrangements (order matters): 4 people in a line = 4! = 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 24
    • Combinations (order doesn't matter): choose 2 of 5 = (5 × 4) ÷ (2 × 1) = 10

    7. Quick check

    Bag: 4 red, 6 blue. P(blue)?

    6/10 = 3/5

    Same bag. P(not blue)?

    1 − 3/5 = 2/5

    Flip two coins. P(both heads)?

    1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4

    Two dice. P(total of 8)?

    (2,6) (3,5) (4,4) (5,3) (6,2) → 5/36

    Derivatives

    A derivative tells you how fast something is changing at one exact moment. On a graph, it's the slope of the curve at a single point, which is the slope of the tangent line that just touches the curve there. A car's speedometer is a derivative: it shows how fast your position is changing right now.

    1. See it: slide along the curve

    Where the curve is flat, at the top of a hill or bottom of a valley, the slope is 0. That's how calculus finds maximums and minimums.

    2. Where it comes from

    Take the slope between two points on the curve, then slide them together until the gap h shrinks to 0:

    f′(x) = limh→0 [f(x + h) − f(x)] ÷ h

    1. f(x) = x²
    2. f(x + h) − f(x) = x² + 2xh + h² − x² = 2xh + h²
    3. Divide by h: 2x + h
    4. Let h → 0: f′(x) = 2x

    You won't do this every time. The rules below are shortcuts.

    3. The basic rules

    • Constant: the derivative of 7 is 0 (a flat line has no slope)
    • Power rule: xⁿ → n·xⁿ⁻¹. Bring the exponent down, subtract 1
    • Constant multiple: 5x³ → 5 · 3x² = 15x²
    • Sum: take each term separately
    1. f(x) = 3x⁴ − 5x² + 7x − 2
    2. 3x⁴ → 12x³; −5x² → −10x; 7x → 7; −2 → 0
    3. f′(x) = 12x³ − 10x + 7

    4. Product, quotient and chain rules

    • Product: (f·g)′ = f′g + fg′
    • Quotient: (f/g)′ = (f′g − fg′) ÷ g²
    • Chain (a function inside a function): derivative of the outside × derivative of the inside
    1. f(x) = (3x + 1)⁵
    2. Outside: 5(3x + 1)⁴. Inside: derivative of 3x + 1 is 3
    3. f′(x) = 15(3x + 1)⁴
    1. f(x) = x² · sin x
    2. f′g + fg′ = 2x · sin x + x² · cos x

    5. Derivatives to know

    f(x)f′(x)
    c (constant)0

    sin xcos x
    cos x−sin x
    eˣeˣ

    6. What derivatives are used for

    Tangent line

    1. f(x) = x² at x = 3. Point: f(3) = 9
    2. Slope: f′(x) = 2x → f′(3) = 6
    3. y − 9 = 6(x − 3) → y = 6x − 9

    Lowest or highest point

    1. f(x) = x² − 4x + 1. Set f′(x) = 2x − 4 = 0
    2. x = 2: the bottom of the curve, f(2) = −3

    Speed

    1. A dropped ball falls s(t) = 16t² feet after t seconds
    2. Speed: s′(t) = 32t → at 3 seconds, 96 feet per second

    7. Quick check

    d/dx of 5x³

    15x²

    d/dx of x² + 4x − 9

    2x + 4

    d/dx of (2x − 1)³

    3(2x − 1)² · 2 = 6(2x − 1)²

    Slope of y = x² at x = −1

    2(−1) = −2
